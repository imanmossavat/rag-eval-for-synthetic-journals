synthetic data · testing RAG and chatbot memory

Does your chatbot actually remember?

A chatbot that answers from your notes can sound right and still be wrong. The usual test is to ask it a few questions and read the replies. This page shows a better way. Write the facts yourself first, so every test question has a known answer and a known proof.

We built one fictional person’s diary for a month, asked 26 questions at five levels of difficulty, and ran four small chatbots on it. The chatbots differ only in how they remember. Every answer was scored by plain code, with no AI judge.

1 · the problem

Real data can’t tell you three things

The right answer

Nobody wrote an answer key for your documents, so a person has to judge every reply. Or another AI does, which brings its own errors.

How hard a question is

Easy look-ups and tricky multi-step questions come mixed together. You can’t turn the difficulty up one step at a time.

Where it went wrong

You don’t know which notes should have been found, so you can’t tell “search missed it” from “the model misread it.”

Those two failures need opposite fixes. A search miss is fixed in the retrieval step; a misreading is fixed in the model or the prompt. If you can’t tell them apart, you change the wrong thing.

2 · the idea

Facts first, text last

Flip the usual order. First write down the hidden facts. Then turn each fact into a diary note. Then build the questions from the facts, with plain code. The answer key exists before a single sentence of text does, so nobody has to work it out from the text afterwards.

Why bother? The project this demo came out of did it the usual way. One AI call wrote each question and its answer from finished text. About 17 to 20% of those answers turned out to be wrong, and nobody noticed for weeks. An AI checker added later was right only 4 times out of 6.

3 · the month

The facts are built in pairs on purpose. Two people have dogs, two have favourite cafés, two have cars. Each fact is the other one’s look-alike: a note that looks like the answer but isn’t.

4 · writing the notes

Every note gets a plan, and code checks it

Each fact becomes a plan: what the note must say, which exact words it must contain, and which words it must not contain. The forbidden list is every other name, relation, value and habit word, so a note can’t leak an answer that belongs elsewhere. A big model writes the note; a few lines of code check it.

the plan for note N027

Say: Femke’s dog’s name is Biscotti.

Must contain: Femke, Biscotti

Must not contain: sister, Pickle, Kofi, bouldering, … 19 more

Leaving out “sister” is what makes the question two-hop. To answer “my sister’s dog” you need this note and the note that says Femke is the sister.

first draft · rejected by the checker

…He tried to eat a whole herring from the fish stall and got very offended when he wasn’t allowed. Biscotti and Pickle sniffed each other for ages and then decided they were best friends.

fail contains forbidden “Pickle”

The draft named the writer’s own dog, the look-alike answer, in the note about Femke’s dog. The fix was one word: “my dog”. The slip happened while the demo data was written by hand. No log of the draft was kept, but tests/test_demo.py replays it against the checker.

59 notes

29 carry a fact (one fact each) and 30 are filler about weather, cooking or a TV series. The filler contains no names and no answers. It is the haystack that search has to dig through.

5 · the questions

Five levels, each a known memory skill

Templates turn the facts into questions. The levels follow the skills that published long-memory tests measure (LongMemEval, LoCoMo, BABILong). The proof column is the number of notes a bot needs to see to answer.

The same fact at two difficulties. “What is Femke’s dog’s name?” and “What is my sister’s dog’s name?” have the same answer. Only the path differs. If the first passes and the second fails, the problem is the join, not the fact.

Traps built in on purpose: look-alike values (two dogs, two cars), stale values (the old phone is still in the diary), a count bigger than the number of notes search returns (7 bouldering trips, 5 slots), and unanswerable questions about a thing someone else has (Femke’s car, when two other people’s cars are in the notes).

6 · the chatbots

Four ways to remember, one prompt

All four bots get the same instruction: answer from the memory only, short answer, or say exactly “I don’t know”. What changes is the memory the model is shown when a question arrives. Each bot is about 20 lines of Python.

Full context

Every note, in date order, pasted into the prompt. No search at all.

shows 2,804 words

Like “just paste it all in”. Only works while everything fits.

Vector search

Standard RAG. Notes become vectors, and the 5 most similar to the question are shown.

shows ~270 words

The usual LlamaIndex or LangChain setup.

Rolling summary

One summary, rewritten after every note, asked to stay under 250 words.

shows 395 words

Like the “memory” in many chat apps.

Fact list

After every note, the model writes down its facts as short dated lines. All lines are kept.

shows 2,748 words

The simplest version of Mem0-style memory.

Two local models answer, from the same family: qwen3:4b (small, 2.5 GB) and qwen3:30b (large, 18 GB). Both run on a laptop through Ollama, with randomness switched off. The large one is a “mixture of experts”: it holds 30 billion parameters but uses only about 3 billion for each word it writes.

The summary and fact-list memories have to be built one note at a time, and that took about an hour each on the small model. To keep the demo short we ran those two on the small model only. Full context and vector search ran on both.

7 · scoring without a judge

Two questions per answer

Because every answer is a short exact string (a name, a brand, a number), a plain text match grades it. No AI judge is needed, so the score is the same every time you run it.

Did search fetch the right notes? vector search only

measurewhat it means
precision@5Of the 5 notes fetched, the share that are proof. Low means junk crowds the prompt. With one proof note it can never beat 0.20.
recall@5Of the proof notes, the share that were fetched. Low means the evidence never reached the model, and no prompt can fix that.
hit@5At least one proof note was fetched. Often reported as “context recall”.
all@5Every proof note was fetched. This is what two-hop, count and history questions actually need.
MRR1 ÷ the position of the first proof note, averaged. 1.0 means the top result is always a proof note.

Was the answer right, and if not, why?

Each answer gets one label. The two kinds of “I don’t know” are the most useful split on this page:

reading failure

Said “I don’t know”, but the answer was in what the model was shown. Fix the model or the prompt.

memory failure

Said “I don’t know”, and the answer was not in what it was shown. Fix search or memory, because a better model can’t help.

The other labels: correct, partial (a list missing items), trap worked (named a look-alike or out-of-date value), wrong, made it up (answered an unanswerable question), and rambled (ignored the short-answer format).

“Rambled” exists because one reply ran to about 13,000 words of the model thinking out loud. It mentioned nearly every name in the diary, so a simple text match would have marked it correct.

9 · finding one

At this size, the whole diary beats any memory

26 / 26

The large model with the full diary in its prompt got every question right. The small model got 24, with two misses we can name: it gave the previous workplace when asked for the current one, and it left the bakery out of the workplace history.

The two memories that compress did much worse on the same small model: 11 of 26 for the rolling summary, 18 of 26 for the fact list. With 59 short notes, the whole diary fits in the prompt easily, so compressing it only throws things away.

This flips when the diary outgrows the model’s window. Then full context isn’t possible at all, and the other three designs are the only options. Regenerating with generate.py --filler builds a bigger haystack to test where that happens (it rewrites the notes, so it needs the API or the manual steps).

10 · finding two

Search always found something, but not everything

By the common measure, search looks perfect: for every question it fetched at least one proof note (hit@5 = 1.00). The stricter measure tells a different story. Questions that need several notes often got only some of them.

hit@5: at least one proof note all@5: every proof note

Counting broke exactly as designed. Noor went bouldering 7 times. Search hands over 5 notes. Both models answered 5: they counted correctly what they were shown. The swimming count (3 trips) was right, because 3 fits in 5 slots.

Two-hop broke on the link. For “What is my roommate’s car?” search found the note about Kofi’s Volvo but not the note saying Kofi is the roommate. Both models said “I don’t know”, which is honest. The text check counts this as “the answer was in memory” because the word Volvo was there, but the link wasn’t. A string being present isn’t the same as the fact being usable.

Right without proof. Three times a model answered a two-hop question correctly although search never fetched the note naming the relation (“my colleague’s café” for both models, “my roommate’s home town” for the large one). The guess was good, but a guess isn’t retrieval. Only all@5 shows that these correct answers rest on missing evidence.

11 · finding three

Compressed memory loses meaning, not just words

rolling summary · 4b · 11 / 26

“…Femke brought stroopwafels and Biscotti. Counted flour sacks with Tomasz… Museum Speelklok visit (March 26)…”

Asked to stay under 250 words, it wrote 395, and it still lost what mattered. “Biscotti” survived but became something Femke brought, not her dog. The new job became a museum visit, so “What is my workplace now?” got the answer “shop”. The words sister, colleague, neighbour and roommate do not appear once, so 5 of 6 two-hop questions failed.

fact list · 4b · 18 / 26

“2026-03-15 | First day at the Openbare Bibliotheek
2026-03-26 | Started at Museum Speelklok
…349 lines…”

The list kept almost everything, and it was nearly as long as the diary itself (2,748 words against 2,804). Yet the small model did worse with it than with the raw notes. Asked for the current workplace, it picked the older line. Nothing in a flat list marks a fact as out of date, and 349 short lines are harder to read than 59 stories. Twice the stale-value or look-alike trap worked.

These two designs are the simplest versions of real products. The project behind this demo tried both for real and had the same trouble: the fact extractor (Mem0) dropped the content that mattered, and the rolling summary kept shrinking to the latest turn.

12 · finding four

A bigger model fixes reading, not retrieval

24 → 26

full context, small → large. The two reading mistakes disappear.

22 → 23

vector search, small → large. The count is still 5, the history is still incomplete, and the roommate’s car is still unknown.

A model can’t read a note it was never shown. When the evidence is missing from the prompt, a better model has nothing to work with. This is why the reading-failure vs memory-failure split matters: it tells you whether to spend money on a bigger model or on better search.

13 · finding five

No bot made up an answer

All 18 attempts at the three unanswerable questions (3 questions × 6 bot-and-model pairs) correctly said “I don’t know”, including the traps about Femke’s car with two other cars in the notes. Here the abstention traps did not fire. Our answering prompt pushes hard for “I don’t know”. That has a cost: 20 of the wrong answers on this page were refusals of questions that could be answered.

14 · lessons

Five things to take home

  1. Write the facts before the text. An answer key worked out from finished text needs checking forever. One written first is exact.
  2. Score per question type, never as one number. One average hides the structure you built. 22 of 26 sounds fine until you see that vector search miscounted bouldering, and missed part of the workplace history, with both models.
  3. Measure “all the proof”, not “some proof”. Hit@5 was a perfect 1.00 while 5 of the 12 multi-note questions were missing at least one proof note.
  4. Split every failure into reading vs memory. They point at opposite fixes: a better model, or better search.
  5. Build traps on purpose. Look-alikes, stale values and counts larger than K showed each design’s weak spot within 26 questions.
15 · limits

What this demo does not show

  • 26 questions and one run each. A difference of one or two answers between bots is within luck. The patterns that repeat (counting, two-hop links, the summary’s losses) are the findings.
  • The diary is small. Full context wins here because everything fits; with thousands of notes the ranking changes.
  • The summary and fact-list bots ran on the small model only.
  • The diary was written by the same AI that designed the test (Claude, in a Claude Code session), with code checking every note. Notes from a different writer could be harder or easier.
  • Short factual answers only. Open questions like “how did I feel this month?” need a different kind of scoring.
